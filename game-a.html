Project Market Lab

Project market, Game A: one firm per round

There are two games. In Game A (this page, the market lab) one firm is sold per round and the operator can switch many rules. Game B (open Game B) sells every firm on offer at once in each round: players bid for up to three firms and can pay a secret boost.

One firm is sold each round, most wanted first unless the operator decides otherwise. Every player still without a firm bids or passes; one player wins; the rest pay rent and wait for the next firm. To try different market designs, open "Change the rules" further down the page. Your score is the value of the firm you win (from your survey ranking, 0 to 100) plus any leftover points the rules say are worth keeping. Nothing here is saved or counts.

You

You are playing as You, highlighted in yellow in every table. The seven other players (Player 1 to Player 7) are simulated. This practice game is for one student on their own; in class the auction is run for groups.

Where the values come from: in this practice game you and every rival have a made-up survey ranking of seven firms (more popular firms are more likely to be picked). Your 1st choice is worth 100 to you, your 2nd 86, and so on down to 14 for your 7th; firms you did not rank are worth 0. Open "Your values" to see your made-up ranking. In the group auction in class, a group's values come from its members' real survey answers.

Market rules

Before the auction, every student has already ranked their seven preferred firms in the survey. These rankings give each player a value for each firm (their favourite firm is 100). The value sets the player's score, and the operator can also use it to weight bids by alignment (Alignment weighting) or to break ties (Most aligned player wins). In this practice lab the rankings are made up; in the real auction they are your survey answers.

Be the operator

Run 1,000 whole auctions with eight simulated players under the market rules set above. Each run adds a row, so you can compare designs. Which rules earn the platform most? Which give players the firms they want? Can you have both?

The simulated players follow fixed strategies (they do not learn), but they do respond to the rules: they never bid above the cap or below the minimum, and when leftover points are worth something they bid less. Ask what real players would do differently.

What the settings mean, and where they are used

Each setting is explained in its own box: first what it is and why it matters, then the choices. Remember that players have already ranked their preferred firms in the survey before the auction starts, so the operator knows how much each player wants each firm and can use that to price firms, weight bids or break ties. Each choice copies a design used by a real market or platform. The examples are illustrations for discussion, not endorsements, and many real markets mix several designs.

How firms are sold

Format

What it is. How a firm changes hands in each round: secret bids, a falling price, or tickets in a draw.

Why it matters. The format decides what players know about each other while bidding and who pays. It changes how cautious or aggressive players are, and how much the operator collects.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
Sealed bidsEach player submits one secret bid; the highest wins.Government contracts and procurement tenders; Google and other ad exchanges, which run a sealed auction for every ad shown.
Falling clock (Dutch)The price starts high and falls until someone stops it; that buyer pays the price at that moment.Dutch flower auctions (Royal FloraHolland, Aalsmeer); fish markets; Google's 2004 share listing used a modified Dutch auction.
Lottery ticketsPoints buy tickets; the winner is drawn in proportion to tickets; everyone pays.Raffles and prize draws; loot boxes in games; some blockchains pick who adds the next block in proportion to the coins they stake.

Price (sealed bids)

What it is. What the winner, and sometimes the losers, actually pay once the bids are opened.

Why it matters. The price rule decides whether it is safe to bid what a firm is really worth to you. It is also the operator's biggest lever on revenue: in the simulations, all-pay raises the operator's share of the points from about 57% (second price) to 88%.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
Second-highest bid + 1The winner pays just enough to beat the runner-up, so bidding what it is really worth to you is safe.eBay's automatic bidding (you pay one step above the next bid); Google's search-ad auction (a second-price design for many years).
Pay your own bidThe winner pays what it bid, so bidders shade their bids down.Most display-ad exchanges since Google Ad Manager switched in 2019; many tenders and some government bond auctions.
Everyone pays their bidLosers lose their bids too (an all-pay contest).Lobbying and political campaign spending; races to a patent; penny auctions, where every bid costs money.

If the top bids tie

What it is. Who gets the firm when two or more players make the same highest bid.

Why it matters. Ties are common when bids are round numbers or players go all in. The tie rule quietly decides who the market favours: the fast, the patient, the keen, the rich or the lucky. Knowing the rule changes how players bid.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
Fastest bid winsEarlier is better when bids are equal.Stock exchanges match orders at the same price by time of arrival (price-time priority), which rewards high-frequency traders; first-come ticket queues.
Tie voids the saleNobody wins; the item goes back on sale.Auction lots that fail to sell are withdrawn and relisted; tied tenders are re-run.
Most rent paid winsRewards those who have waited longest or paid most.Waiting lists and housing queues ordered by time waiting; loyalty and status upgrades on airlines.
Most aligned player winsOf the tied players, the one with the highest value for the firm (its alignment, from the survey) gets it.Matching by stated preference, as in school and university admissions; relevance ranking on platforms.
Most points left winsThe richest bidder wins ties.Large investors getting priority allocations in share listings; staking systems where more coins mean more influence.
Re-offer with a higher minimumCompetition raises the reserve price next time.Relisting on eBay with a higher reserve; airlines and ticket sellers raising prices when demand shows.
Random drawChance decides.London Marathon and Wimbledon ballots; lotteries for oversubscribed school places in some areas.

Limits on bids

Bid cap

What it is. The largest share of its current points a player may bid on any one firm.

Why it matters. Without a cap, keen players can go all in on the first firm and the auction becomes a contest of nerve. A cap spreads spending across rounds, but also lowers what the operator earns.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
No capA player can bid all its points.Most open auctions; high-stakes bidding wars.
75%, 60% or 50% of pointsNo bid above that share of your points.Campaign spending limits; daily budget caps in Google Ads; limits of a few tickets per customer.

Bids in steps of

What it is. The size of the jumps between allowed bids (for example 10, 20, 30).

Why it matters. Big steps make ties more likely, which brings the tie rule into play; small steps let players outbid each other by a single point.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
10Bids must be multiples of 10; ties are common.Bid increments at auction houses and on eBay.
5 or 1Finer bids; fewer ties.Minimum price steps (tick sizes) on stock exchanges.

Minimum bid

What it is. The lowest bid accepted for any firm (a reserve price).

Why it matters. A reserve stops firms being sold for almost nothing and guarantees the operator some income from every sale.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
1, or any number up to 50Nothing sells below this.Reserve prices at auction houses and on eBay; floor prices in ad exchanges.

Minimum price from survey demand

What it is. A starting price for each firm set from how popular it was in the survey: the most wanted firm costs most.

Why it matters. The operator already knows demand from the survey, so it can charge more for popular firms before any bid is made. This raises revenue and steers some players towards less wanted firms.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
OffEvery firm has the same minimum.Fixed-price sales.
Top firm starts at 20, 40 or 60Popular firms start at a higher price; others in proportion to their demand.Surge pricing at Uber; dynamic ticket pricing (the 2024 Oasis ticket sale); airline fares that rise with demand.

Alignment weighting

What it is. Alignment is how much a player wants a firm: its value for that firm, worked out from their survey ranking, on a scale where the player's favourite firm is 100. With weighting on, a bid counts as bid × (0.5 + value ÷ 100). A player that values the firm at 100 has its bid counted at 1.5 times; at 50, at face value; at 10, at only 0.6 times. Example: Player A bids 40 and values the firm at 90, so its bid counts as 40 × 1.4 = 56. Player B bids 50 but values the firm at only 30, so its bid counts as 50 × 0.8 = 40. Player A wins with the smaller bid. Players always pay in real points, not counted points: with second price, Player A pays just enough for its bid to count more than 40, about 30 points.

Why it matters. Without weighting, the richest or boldest player wins whether or not it really wants the firm. Weighting rewards players that care most about a firm, and it cuts ties, because equal bids rarely count the same. But the platform decides how much fit counts and measures it with data it holds (here the survey), so it can favour some bidders over others in ways bidders cannot see or check. The same idea appears in the tie rule 'Most aligned player wins', which uses alignment only to break ties.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
OffOnly the size of the bid counts.Simple highest-bid auctions.
OnA bid counts more if the player values the firm more (bid × quality), so a keen player can beat a richer one.Google ranks ads by bid × quality score; Amazon and app stores weigh relevance as well as payment when ranking sponsored listings.

Rent

Rent per round

What it is. Points every player still without a firm pays the operator each round, whether it bid or passed.

Why it matters. Rent makes waiting costly, so it pushes players to bid early instead of holding out for a cheap firm. It is steady income for the operator whatever happens in the bidding.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
10 (or any number up to 40)Every player without a firm pays this each round.Seller subscriptions (Amazon's monthly fee for professional sellers); Apple's yearly developer fee; storage and listing fees.

Rent rises each round by

What it is. How much the rent goes up each round.

Why it matters. Rising rent makes each extra round of waiting dearer than the last, which hurts patient players most.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
0Rent stays the same.Flat fees.
5 or 10Waiting gets more expensive each round.Amazon's storage surcharges that rise the longer stock sits in its warehouses; escalating late fees.

Most rent a player can pay

What it is. The total rent any one player can be charged over the whole auction.

Why it matters. A rent cap limits what the operator can extract from players that keep losing, and protects them from being drained before they win anything.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
30, 50 or 80Rent stops once a player has paid this much.Caps on card fees (the EU limits interchange fees); Scotland's temporary rent cap of 2022.
No limitRent continues until the player wins.Uncapped fees and rents.

The market

Firms on offer

What it is. How many firms are put up for sale (the most wanted in the survey).

Why it matters. This sets how scarce the goods are. With few firms, players fight over the same ones; with many, most players can find a firm they like without paying much.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
8, 10, 12 or 15Fewer firms means more competition and higher prices.Spectrum auctions for a fixed number of mobile licences; limited-edition drops.

Order of sale

What it is. Which firm is sold first, second and so on.

Why it matters. If the most wanted firm comes first, players fight hard at the start; if it comes last, they pay rent while they wait. The order shapes who fights, who waits and how much the operator earns.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
Most wanted firstThe star lot opens the sale.Auction houses placing star lots in the running order.
Least wanted firstPopular firms come at the end, after players have paid rent.Draft order in sports leagues, where the weakest pick first.
Random orderNobody can plan around the order.Random draws for running orders.

Leftover points are worth

What it is. Whether the points a player has left at the end add to its score.

Why it matters. If leftover points are worthless, there is no reason to save them and players spend everything. If they count, every point spent has to be worth it, so bids fall.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
NothingPoints not spent are lost.Air miles, gift cards and in-game currencies that expire or can only be spent in one place.
Half a point or a point eachSaving points pays.Cash, which keeps its value outside any one market.

Week 3 blocking

What it is. Whether each player is barred from the firm it studied in the Week 3 case study.

Why it matters. Blocking makes sure students study something new, and it takes some players out of the race for some firms, which changes the competition for those firms.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
OnEach player has one firm it cannot bid for (you can set yours to none).Conflict-of-interest rules that bar some bidders from some contracts.
OffEvery player can bid for every firm.Open markets.

Side bets

Betting system

What it is. How bets on the outcome of the auction are priced and paid out. Betting uses separate tokens that never affect who gets a firm.

Why it matters. The system decides whether odds are set by the crowd's stakes at the end or by live prices that move as people buy, and so how much the operator can win or lose.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
Pooled bettingWinners share the pot after the operator's cut.The Tote in British horse racing and other pari-mutuel pools.
Live pricesAn automated market maker sets prices that move as people buy.Prediction markets such as Polymarket and Kalshi; automated market makers on crypto exchanges such as Uniswap.

Operator's cut

What it is. The share of each bet or purchase the operator keeps.

Why it matters. The cut is the platform's guaranteed income from betting; a higher cut means smaller payouts for bettors.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
5% (0 to 30%)The platform's share of every bet or trade.Bookmakers' margins; betting-exchange commission (Betfair); the App Store's commission on sales.
