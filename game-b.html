Project market, Game B: several firms per round

There are two games. In Game B (this page) every firm on offer is sold at once in each round: each team bids for up to three firms, the biggest bids are settled first, and losing teams pay rent and bid again. Game A (the market lab, file UDC_W3_market_lab_v12.html) sells one firm per round, with more rules to switch. In both, teams have already ranked their preferred firms in the survey before the auction.

Try bidding strategies for the group project firms against six simulated teams before the real auction. Nothing here is saved or counts. The rules: sealed bids, the biggest bids win first, winners pay the second-highest bid plus 1, losing teams pay rent and bid again.

Be the operator: set the rules, collect the revenue

Now run the market yourself. Choose the rules, then run 1,000 simulated auctions with seven teams playing a mix of strategies. See how much the operator collects, where it comes from, and what your rules do to the teams. Run several versions; each one is added to the table so you can compare them. Can you find the rules that earn the most? And what do they cost the teams?

The simulated teams do not change their strategy when you change the rules: each one keeps bidding the way its strategy says. Real teams would adapt, for example bidding lower when they must pay their own bid. Ask what your best rules would do once teams learned them.

What the settings mean, and where they are used

Each setting of Game B is explained in its own box: first what it is and why it matters, then the choices and where they are used in real markets. The examples are illustrations for discussion, not endorsements.

How firms are sold

Price

What it is. What a winning team pays for its firm.

Why it matters. The price rule decides whether it is safe to bid what a firm is really worth to you, and how much the operator collects.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
Second-highest bid + 1The winner pays just enough to beat the best rival bid for that firm, never more than its own bid.eBay's automatic bidding; Google's search-ad auction (a second-price design for many years).
Pay your own bidThe winner pays what it bid, so teams shade their bids down.Most display-ad exchanges since Google Ad Manager switched in 2019; many tenders.

Bids per round

What it is. How many firms a team can bid for in one round (each with its own amount).

Why it matters. All firms are sold at once, and only one bid per team can win. More bids give a team more chances, but its points are spread thinner, and its largest bid is settled first.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
3Up to three firms per round.Combinatorial and multi-item auctions, such as spectrum auctions where bidders bid for several licences at once.
1, 2, 4 or 5Fewer bids force teams to commit; more let them hedge.Application limits, such as five choices on a UCAS form.

Rounds before firms go by lot

What it is. How many rounds of bidding there are before any team still without a firm is given one at random.

Why it matters. Few rounds mean losing teams soon get a free firm, which makes waiting attractive; more rounds mean more rent for the operator.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
3After three rounds, remaining teams get a free firm by lot.Clearing rounds that end in a ballot, such as university Clearing or housing allocation.
1, 2 or 5Shorter or longer markets.Sales with a fixed closing time.

Limits on bids

Minimum bid

What it is. The lowest bid accepted for any firm (a reserve price).

Why it matters. A reserve stops firms selling for almost nothing. Without one, an uncontested firm costs 1 point.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
1, or any number up to 50Nothing sells below this.Reserve prices at auction houses and on eBay; floor prices in ad exchanges.

Secret boost

What it is. Points a team pays the operator, in secret, to make all its bids rank as if they were bigger. The multiplier is 1 + boost ÷ 50 (with the default strength), so a 10-point boost ranks bids at 1.2 times. The boost is paid whether the team wins or not.

Why it matters. It is a pay-to-rank deal: the team pays the platform for visibility, not for the firm. It can turn a close loss into a win, or save points on the price, but it is wasted if the firm was uncontested or the team loses anyway. It is pure profit for the operator.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
Up to 25 pointsThe largest boost a team may buy.Promoted listings and sponsored posts; paying for priority boarding or queue jumping.
Boost strengthHow much each boost point is worth: 2.5, 5 (default) or 10 points for each +0.1 on the multiplier.Ad platforms changing what a unit of spend buys.

Rent

Rent per round

What it is. Points every team that won nothing in a round pays the operator before bidding again.

Why it matters. Rent makes losing costly: three lost rounds at the default cost 60 points. It pushes teams to bid for firms they can win, and is steady income for the operator.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
20 (or any number up to 50)Each losing team pays this, or all it has left.Seller subscriptions (Amazon's monthly fee for professional sellers); Apple's yearly developer fee.

The market

Firms on offer

What it is. Which firms are up for auction: the most popular N in the survey, every firm someone chose, or all 30.

Why it matters. This sets how scarce the goods are. With few firms, teams fight over the same ones; with many, most teams can find a firm they like cheaply.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
Most popular 10The default.Spectrum auctions for a fixed number of mobile licences; limited-edition drops.
9, 12, 15, 20 or allFewer or more firms.Opening a market wider.

Week 3 blocking

What it is. Teams cannot bid for a firm any member studied in the Week 3 case study.

Why it matters. It makes sure students study something new, and takes some teams out of the race for some firms.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
Your team's blocked firmChoose which firm your team cannot bid for (or none).Conflict-of-interest rules that bar some bidders from some contracts.

Side bets

Betting system

What it is. How bets on the outcome are priced and paid out. Betting uses separate tokens that never affect who gets a firm.

Why it matters. It decides whether odds come from the crowd's stakes or from live prices, and how much the operator can win or lose.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
Pooled bettingWinners share the pot after the operator's cut.The Tote in British horse racing and other pari-mutuel pools.
Live pricesAn automated market maker sets prices that move as people buy.Prediction markets such as Polymarket and Kalshi.

Operator's cut

What it is. The share of each bet or purchase the operator keeps.

Why it matters. The cut is the platform's guaranteed income from betting; a higher cut means smaller payouts.

ChoiceWhat it doesWhere it is used
5% (0 to 30%)The platform's share of every bet or trade.Bookmakers' margins; betting-exchange commission (Betfair); the App Store's commission.
